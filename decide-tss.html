Decide: Target Selection Standards

You have decided what to attack and how it will be attacked. Now you decide how good a report must be before you are willing to act on it.

Target selection standards are the criteria a target must meet before we decide to prosecute it. They are set in advance, while we still have time to weigh how much ammunition we hold and the risk of being detected when we shoot. A target that meets the criteria is engaged. A target that does not can be monitored, passed to a subordinate unit, or taken back to the commander for further guidance. Concept from MCTP 3-31B, Appendix A (TSS is a product of the decide function).

In this scenario we will use three standards.

StandardThe question it asksWhat determines the answer
Target location error (TLE)How accurate is the location of the target?The weapon. Some weapons require precise coordinates to be effective. Others are “area fire”.
Minimum sizeIs there enough of the target to be worth what we will spend?The payoff. What do we gain, against what we fire?
TimelinessHow long does the answer to the first two questions stay true?The target's ability to move, or our weapon's ability to guide to the target.

Read them in that order. The first two describe the report at the moment it was made. The third is the clock running on both of them.

Standard 1 — Target location error

Target location error is the gap between where we think the target is and where it actually is, measured in metres. It comes from the sensor, not from the target. A forward observer may be capable of locating a target within 100 metres. A drone sensor may be accurate within 10 metres, for example.

The weapon decides how much error it can absorb. The bigger the shell and the more guns firing, the more location error the mission can survive.

WeaponLargest location error it can accept
155 mm M109A2 battery200 m
105 mm howitzer battery100 m
One-way attack UAS1,000 m

These figures are set for this scenario. They are not published doctrine. The location error a weapon can accept depends on several factors, including its circular error probable, the terrain, and the number of rounds in a given volley.

The 105 mm howitzer battery accepts less error than the 155 mm howitzer, because a 105 mm shell covers less ground than a 155 mm shell. The same report can therefore be good enough for one weapon and useless for another.

Standard 3 — Timeliness

Timeliness is the clock running on the other two standards. A location that was exact an hour ago describes a place the target has left. A size that was right an hour ago describes a platoon that has since dispersed.

Some targets we watch continuously. For those, timeliness is zero: we fire while we still have the target in sight, and the clock starts the moment we lose it. For the rest, the brigade uses a rule:

Extract — 77th Brigade Fire Support SOP

Setting timeliness. Intelligence tells us how often an enemy element relocates. He relocates on a schedule, because he cannot know when we have found him. What intelligence cannot tell us is when he arrived. Our report may have caught him in his first hour in position, or his last.

To manage that risk, this brigade sets timeliness at one quarter of the time the element is expected to stay in place. This is not a guarantee. It is the commander accepting that some missions will land on empty ground, in exchange for being able to engage targets that nobody is watching at this moment.

Where an element moves as soon as it is used — a mortar platoon after firing, a reconnaissance team on foot — no fraction is useful. Those targets require continuous observation.

All three at once

In the fire support cell no report is checked against one standard. It is checked against all of them, and it must pass every one.

Why is the most precise weapon given the loosest standard?

Target location error was invented for guns. A gun fires where you aim it, so the error in the grid must be smaller than the ground the shells cover.

A one-way attack UAS is flown. It is launched toward an area, it searches, and the operator puts it onto the target he can see. The grid does not have to be exact. It only has to be small enough for the drone to search in the time it can stay in the air.

So a 1,000 metre area is acceptable: what we are spending is the drone's flying time, not the accuracy of the grid.

Units set this number differently, because target selection standards were written before these aircraft existed. Ask what your own unit uses.
