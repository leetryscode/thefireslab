Decide: Target Selection Standards

You have decided what to attack and how it will be attacked. Now you decide how good a report must be before you are willing to act on it.

Target selection standards are the criteria a target must meet before we decide to prosecute it. They are set in advance, while we still have time to weigh how much ammunition we hold and the risk of being detected when we shoot. A target that meets the criteria is engaged. A target that does not can be monitored, passed to a subordinate unit, or taken back to the commander for further guidance. Concept from MCTP 3-31B, Appendix A (TSS is a product of the decide function).

In this scenario we will use three standards.

StandardThe question it asksWhat determines the answer
Target location error (TLE)How accurate is the location of the target?The weapon. Some weapons require precise coordinates to be effective. Others are “area fire”.
Minimum sizeIs the target big enough to be worth the resources?The payoff. What do we gain, against what we fire?
TimelinessIf we lose track of the target, how long until the target location is determined to be invalid?The target's ability to move, or our weapon's ability to guide to the target.

Standard 1 — Target location error

Target location error is the gap between where we think the target is and where it actually is, measured in metres. It comes from the sensor, not from the target. A forward observer may be capable of locating a target within 100 metres. A drone sensor may be accurate within 10 metres, for example.

The weapon determines how much error can be accepted. The bigger the shell and the more guns firing, the more location error the mission can survive.

WeaponLargest location error it can accept
M109 155 mm battery200 m
M101 105 mm howitzer battery100 m
One-way attack UAS1,000 m

These figures are set for this scenario. They are not published doctrine. The location error a weapon can accept depends on several factors, including its circular error probable, the terrain, and the number of rounds in a given volley.

The M101 105 mm howitzer battery accepts less error than the 155 mm howitzer, because a 105 mm shell covers less ground than a 155 mm shell. The same report can therefore be good enough for one weapon and useless for another.

All four judged correctly. Minimum size is set by what we gain, not by how many we can count. A report can be fresh and accurate and still not show you the target.

Standard 3 — Timeliness

The timeliness standard only applies if we are not currently observing the target. It describes the amount of time a target can last unobserved before we decide we may not shoot. A target may move to a different location, causing us to miss if we do not continually observe it.

For targets with the ability to move, shooting without constant observation is risky, and the commander must weigh this against missing the opportunity to engage it. For targets that continuously move, the timeliness standard is often zero.

Opportunity to kill the enemy
Wasting ammunition and missing the enemy

Task 1.10 — Approve the fire mission

NOT DONE

Three reports have arrived. Check each one against the worksheet above and decide what to engage it with. A weapon is only an option if the report clears every standard in that weapon's block. Check each row as you go.

1. 120 mm mortar platoon
An enemy mortar position, spotted 15 minutes ago by long-range ISR that has since flown home to recharge its batteries. Location accurate to 50 m.

The location is not the problem. 50 m is inside every location error on the worksheet, so all three weapons would accept the grid. Timeliness is what fails. The mortar platoon is allowed 5 minutes against all three weapons, because five minutes is how long it takes them to leave, and this report is already 15 minutes old. The ISR that found them has gone home, so nobody is watching that grid now. Whatever we fire lands on a position they have left. Do not engage: send it back and task a sensor to find them again.

2. Battalion command post
Human intelligence reports the position of enemy command tents, with key leaders seen entering 6 hours ago. Location accurate to 50 m.

Every standard passes, and that is the point of this one. 50 m is inside all three location errors. Command tents with key leaders answer the minimum size, which asks for a command vehicle or a structure. The command post is allowed 12 hours, so a report 6 hours old is still good. When more than one weapon is permitted, the attack order decides. Read the command post column down the location error cells: the M109 155 mm battery carries the 1, so it is the primary means.

3. ATGM section
A small UAS is looking at the ATGM position right now. We are out of one-way attack drones. Location accurate to 200 m.

Timeliness passes: the ATGM section demands continuous observation from a gun, and the small UAS is watching it as the report arrives. Now walk the attack order down that column. The drone is the primary means, and we have none left. The M101 105 mm howitzer battery is next, but it accepts only 100 m and this report is 200 m, so it is out on location error. The M109 155 mm battery accepts 200 m, which this report just meets. That is what the order is for: walk down it until you reach a weapon that is both available and inside its own standards.

Why is the most precise weapon given the loosest standard?

Target location error was invented for guns. A gun fires where you aim it, so the error in the grid must be smaller than the ground the shells cover.

A one-way attack UAS is flown. It is launched toward an area, it searches, and the operator puts it onto the target he can see. The grid does not have to be exact. It only has to be small enough for the drone to search in the time it can stay in the air.

So a 1,000 metre area is acceptable: what we are spending is the drone's flying time, not the accuracy of the grid.

Units set this number differently, because target selection standards were written before these aircraft existed. Ask what your own unit uses.

Why does the drone get a window when the guns do not?

Timeliness asks how long a report stays good enough to act on. Against a gun that is a question about the target alone. A gun fires at a grid, so if the team has moved off that grid, the shells land on empty ground.

A one-way attack UAS is not fired at a grid. It is flown to the area, it searches, and the operator puts it onto the target he can see. A report fifteen minutes old is still worth launching on, because the drone does the finding when it arrives.

So the two targets that demand continuous observation from the guns can be given a fifteen minute window against the drone. It is the same fact that gives the drone the loosest location error on this worksheet: the drone searches, and the gun does not.

Fifteen minutes is set for this scenario, as the location error is. Ask what your own unit uses.
